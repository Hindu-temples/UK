Hinduism in Britain: a short history

Britain's Hindu community grew from roughly 30,000 people in 1961 to over a million by 2021 — and the 154 mandirs in this directory are the physical record of that. Most began as converted church halls, shops and front rooms. This is how they came to be here.

Before the migration

Hindu presence in Britain long predates the post-war era, but organised institutions were rare. In 1929 the scholar Hari Prasad Shastri founded Shanti Sadan in London, one of the earliest lasting Hindu centres in the country. For decades afterwards, worship for the small resident community happened privately.

Three waves

Hindu migration to Britain came in three broad waves. The first, after the Second World War, brought people directly from Gujarat and Punjab to fill labour shortages in industrial towns. The second, and the most consequential, came from East Africa in the late 1960s and early 1970s. The third, more recent, has drawn Hindus from across India and from the wider diaspora — Sri Lanka, Mauritius, Fiji, Guyana, Trinidad — which is why the traditions in this directory range from Gujarati Swaminarayan to Tamil Saiva.

1972: the year that changed the map

In August 1972, President Idi Amin ordered the expulsion of Uganda's Asian population. Around 60,000 people were forced to leave, and the British government admitted some 27,000 through the Uganda Resettlement Board. Many were Gujarati Hindus who arrived with almost nothing, having lost homes and businesses built over generations.

They settled where there was work and where community already existed — Leicester, Wembley and Brent, Coventry, Bolton, Preston. That single event explains much of the map on this site: it is why Leicester's Belgrave Road became the Golden Mile, and why London and the East Midlands hold the densest clusters of mandirs in the country today.

From front rooms to mandirs

The earliest temples were not built as temples. They were disused churches, chapels, warehouses, shops and terraced houses, bought cheaply by communities with little money and consecrated by installing the murtis — because a building becomes a mandir only when the deities are installed in it. That pattern is still visible: many temples in this directory occupy buildings that began life as something else.

Timeline

  1. 1929

    Shanti Sadan opens in London

    Hari Prasad Shastri, a teacher of Adhyatma Yoga, settles in London and founds Shanti Sadan — among the earliest organised Hindu institutions in Britain, decades before mass migration.

  2. 1950s

    The first congregations

    Hindus begin arriving in numbers after the Second World War, mostly from Gujarat and Punjab. Worship happens in front rooms and hired halls; there are no temples yet.

  3. 1960s

    ISKCON reaches London

    Devotees sent from San Francisco establish a base in a Covent Garden warehouse. George Harrison takes an interest, records with them at Abbey Road, and co-signs the lease on their Bloomsbury premises.

  4. 1969

    Leicester: murtis installed on Cromford Street

    On 20 July 1969, Radha-Krishna murtis are installed at a converted property in Highfields, Leicester — widely cited as the first Hindu temple in Britain. A building becomes a mandir only once the murtis are installed. See temples in Leicester →

  5. 1970

    BAPS opens in a disused Islington church

    The Swaminarayan community converts a redundant church in Islington — typical of the era, when almost every early mandir was a repurposed chapel, hall or shop.

  6. 1972

    The Ugandan expulsion

    Idi Amin expels Uganda's Asian population. Around 60,000 people are forced out; the British government admits some 27,000 through the Uganda Resettlement Board. Many are Gujarati Hindus who arrive having lost homes and businesses. Leicester, Wembley and the East Midlands are transformed.

  7. 1973

    George Harrison gives Bhaktivedanta Manor

    Harrison buys the Hertfordshire estate and donates it to ISKCON. It becomes the movement's UK home and today hosts the largest Janmashtami celebration outside India. See temples in Watford →

  8. 1980

    Purpose-run mandirs take shape

    Shri Nathji Sanatan Hindu Mandir opens in Leytonstone under Shri Vallabh Nidhi UK, as communities move from borrowed rooms to buildings of their own.

  9. 1981

    Wimbledon: the first fully consecrated temple

    Shree Ghanapathy Temple opens in a former church hall in Wimbledon, redesigned to Hindu architectural guidelines — described as the first fully consecrated Hindu temple in Europe. See temples in London →

  10. 1982

    Neasden begins

    Having outgrown Islington, BAPS moves to a converted warehouse in Neasden — the site that will become Britain's best-known mandir.

  11. 1995

    Europe's first traditional stone mandir

    On 20 August 1995, BAPS Shri Swaminarayan Mandir opens in Neasden: hand-carved marble and limestone, built by traditional methods at a cost of around £12 million. The first purpose-built stone mandir in Europe, as distinct from a converted secular building. See temples in London →

  12. 2010

    A second generation of building

    Shree Sanatan Hindu Mandir is completed off Ealing Road, Wembley — part of a wave of purpose-built mandirs replacing the converted halls of the 1970s. See temples in London →

  13. 2021

    Over a million

    The census records 1,066,894 Hindus across the UK — the third-largest religious group, and more than triple the 1981 figure.

The numbers

Religious affiliation was not recorded in the census before 2001, so earlier figures are estimates. The trajectory is nonetheless clear.

Greater London alone accounts for over 450,000 Hindus — around 5.1% of its population, the highest concentration in the UK. You can see the same pattern in this directory: London holds more mandirs than any other city, followed by Leicester.

A note on "the first Hindu temple in Britain"

Several temples are described as the first, and they are describing different things. The Leicester mandir of 1969 is generally cited as the earliest Hindu temple in Britain, in a converted property. Shree Ghanapathy Temple in Wimbledon (1981) is described as the first fully consecrated Hindu temple in Europe. BAPS Shri Swaminarayan Mandir at Neasden (1995) was Europe's first traditional stone mandir, purpose-built rather than converted. All three claims can be true at once.
